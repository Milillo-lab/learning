Local incidence angle and terrain effects

In a radar image of hills, the slopes that face the satellite often look bright and the slopes that face away look dark, even when the two sides are the same kind of ground. What changes is the angle at which the radar wave meets each slope. Steer that angle yourself on a cross-section, see it in real NISAR and Sentinel-1 images of the Panamint Range, then move it over real elevation models.

Goes with SAR, Explained #3, “Local incidence angle: when the ground is not flat” (the part from 1:48 to 2:23: why facing slopes look brighter, the slant-range squeeze, layover and shadow).

The ideas in one screen

Incidence angle and local incidence angle

The incidence angle θ is measured at the ground, between straight up (the local vertical) and the line back to the satellite. On a slope, the line perpendicular to the surface tilts with it. The local incidence angle θloc is the angle between that line and the line back to the satellite: the angle at which the wave actually meets the slope.

When a slope tilts straight toward the radar, the local incidence angle shrinks by the slope; when it tilts straight away, it grows by the slope. With an incidence angle of 40° and a 20° slope: facing the radar 20°, facing away 60°. Tilted along the track it changes much less, about 44°.

cos θloc = n̂ · l̂
toward: θloc = θ − α  ·  away: θ + α
along track: cos θloc = cos θ · cos α

n̂: unit vector perpendicular to the slope; l̂: unit vector from the ground to the satellite; α: slope angle.

Why a slope facing the radar usually looks brighter: two reasons

1. Scattering. Unless the ground is very rough, much of the energy carries on forward, away from the radar, the way a low sun glares off a road. The closer to head-on the wave arrives, the larger the share that comes back toward the radar. This page uses the simplest model of a rough surface, Lambert’s law, in which the backscatter falls as cos2θloc. Smoother ground falls off faster with angle; forest in cross-polarization can even rise with angle, which is why the word is “usually”.

2. The squeeze. The radar sorts echoes by their distance. On a slope facing it, the top and the foot are closer in distance than on level ground (the top is still farther away than the foot, only by less), so the slope fits in fewer pixels and the echoes from all of it add up there. Per kilometre of slope, with 40° incidence and a 20° slope: 0.34 km of slant range facing the radar, 0.64 km on level ground, 0.87 km facing away.

slant range per km of slope = sin θloc
brightness vs level = [cos²θloc / cos²θ] × [sin θ / sin θloc]

The second formula is for slopes tilted in the range direction. For any other direction the page uses the projection of the slope onto the radar image plane (the “image area per unit of slope area” in the readouts), which equals sin θloc in the range case. It is a teaching model, not a calibrated one.

Layover and shadow

Layover. If a slope facing the radar is steeper than the incidence angle, its top comes back before its foot. The slope is imaged reversed and on top of the ground in front of it.

Shadow. The slope facing away meets the wave farther from head-on, sends less energy back and spreads over more pixels, so it looks dark. If it drops away more steeply than the line to the satellite, that is, steeper than 90° − θ, the wave cannot reach it and the image holds only noise there.

layover: α > θ (toward)
shadow: α > 90° − θ (away)

At 40° incidence: layover past 40° toward the radar, shadow past 50° away. For a slope that tilts obliquely, use its tilt in the range direction, tanαr = tanα · cosφ, where φ is the angle between the way the slope faces and the direction to the satellite.

Map view, north up. Headings are those of a near-polar orbit at mid latitudes: about 344.5° ascending (measured on the NISAR pass over the Panamint Range below) and about 195.5° descending (its mirror image). Sentinel-1 flies a similar orbit. NISAR looks left of its track and Sentinel-1 looks right, so on the same pass direction they light opposite sides of a ridge.

Simplifications: flat Earth, the satellite far enough away that its rays are parallel (it is about 750 km away), one plane slope between level ground. Brightness uses Lambert’s cos² law and ignores the noise floor, the antenna pattern and anything not on the slope.

Try this

  1. Press Video example. Read the local incidence angle and the slant range per kilometre for the three directions (toward, away, along the track). You should get 20° and 0.34 km, 60° and 0.87 km, and about 44°. Count the equally spaced range lines that each slope crosses.
  2. Keep the slope facing the radar and raise it slowly past 40°. Watch the strip: when does the top land before the foot? Now lower the incidence angle to 30°. At what slope does layover start now?
  3. Make the slope face away and steepen it. Where does shadow start at 40° incidence? At 25°? Which kind of mission (small or large incidence angles) suffers more from shadow, and which from layover?
  4. Make the slope face east (90°) with the ascending pass. Switch the look side from left to right. Then switch the pass. Which two combinations see the same slope as facing the radar? This is why ascending and descending passes see different sides of a mountain.
