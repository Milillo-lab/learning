Learning / Counting fringes

Counting fringes at Ridgecrest

Each band of color in an interferogram is one turn of the phase, half a wavelength of change in the distance to the satellite. The radar does not count the turns; you can. Walk along a line across the real interferogram of the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquakes, mark every place where the colors restart, and watch your count build up the motion, until the ground breaks and counting stops working.

The idea in one equation

The interferogram stores the phase only modulo one turn: a reading between −½ and +½ of a fringe. To recover the motion, start from a point taken as fixed and add one fringe each time the reading restarts:

dLOS(x) = (λ/2) · [ n(x) + φ(x)/2π ]

where φ is the wrapped phase, n(x) the number of restarts crossed between the reference and x (counted with their direction), and λ/2 = for Sentinel-1. This is phase unwrapping. It works as long as neighboring pixels differ by less than half a fringe, so that every restart is a real restart and not noise. Where the ground broke, pixels next to each other moved by more than that, or the phase turned to noise, and the count can no longer be trusted.

Walk through it

1. From A to the gray bandStart at A, on the left of the profile chart, and click each restart until the gray band begins. The direction of each restart sets the sign: a jump down of the reading means the ground kept moving toward the satellite past half a fringe, so one fringe is added; a jump up subtracts one. On the profile of the post the restarts add up to a net six fringes away from the satellite, and your total at the edge of the band sits on the blue line, about 17 cm away from the satellite.
2. The other side of the bandNow mark the restarts between the far edge of the band and B, and leave the band itself alone. Your orange line on that side runs parallel to the blue one, shifted by a whole number of fringes: the fringes inside the band that you did not count. Look closely at the first few pixels past the band. The fringes there are so tight that one 90 m pixel holds more than half a fringe, so a restart can hide between two pixels and the count slips by one. Depending on where you take the edge, the ground just past the band sits 40 to 50 cm closer to the satellite than B, some fifteen to seventeen fringes.
3. Across the bandPress Count for me. The page marks every jump larger than half a fringe along the whole line, the simplest unwrapping rule there is. Inside the gray band the jumps come in noisy bursts, and after the band the orange line no longer follows the blue one: on the profile of the post the two disagree at B by dozens of fringes. The ARIA product, unwrapped with a more careful algorithm (SNAPHU), does not settle the question either: in the band it is guessing too, and its value across the band is not a measurement.
4. A longer wavelengthSwitch the wrap to NISAR L band. The motion is the same; only the length of a fringe changes, from 2.77 to 12.10 cm, so there are about a quarter as many restarts to count, and fewer of them crowd together near the fault. Count for me still goes wrong across the band, but on the profile of the post by five fringes instead of dozens. This is the Sentinel-1 motion rewrapped, not NISAR data: NISAR did not fly in 2019, and its longer wave would also keep more coherence in the broken ground, which this page does not model.

Try this

  1. Move A next to the fault, inside the band, and count toward B. What happens to your total, and why is the reference point usually chosen far from the deformation?
  2. Draw a profile that stays on one side of the fault, from the southwest corner toward the west edge of the band. Does Count for me now agree with ARIA all the way?
  3. With the Sentinel-1 wrap, find the place along the profile where two restarts are closest together. How far apart are they, and how many pixels does that make? What does the rule "less than half a fringe between neighbors" say about a 90 m pixel there?
  4. Compare the count from A to the band at C band and at L band. Is the L-band count exactly the C-band count times 2.77/12.10? Why only approximately?

How it was made

Data: the ARIA Sentinel-1 geocoded unwrapped interferogram (GUNW, version 3.0.1) of descending track 71, 4 and 16 July 2019, with its coherence and connected components, cropped to 35.40° to 36.04° N and 118.00° to 117.20° W on the product's 0.000833° (about 90 m) grid. The unwrapped phase u is converted to a change in distance toward the satellite with d = λu/4π, with λ = 5.5466 cm from the product metadata (ARIA convention: later date first, positive toward the satellite), and stored losslessly to 0.0014 cm. The profile of the post runs from A (35.52° N, 117.80° W) to B (35.92° N, 117.40° W); values along it are read from the nearest pixel. On that profile the page reproduces the numbers of Figure 3 of Radar, Explained #2: 17.4 cm away from the satellite from A to the band, 46.2 cm toward it from B to the band. The NISAR wrap uses λ = 24.20 cm, from the center frequency of 1239 MHz of NISAR's frequency A in the 40+5 MHz mode (ASF NISAR Data User Guide, Table 1). The data preparation script is tools/make_learning_data.py in the Radar, Explained #2 project.
