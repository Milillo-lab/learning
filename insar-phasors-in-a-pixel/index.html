Phasors in a pixel

One pixel of a radar image is not one echo. It is the sum of the echoes of every object inside the resolution cell, each an arrow in the complex plane. Move the objects, move the radar, let the leaves stir, and watch why the phase of one image is random while the phase difference of two images is not.

The idea in one equation

Each scatterer k at range Rk returns a phasor of amplitude ak and phase −4πRk/λ. The pixel stores their sum,

s = Σk ak exp(−j 4π Rk / λ)

A range difference of a quarter wavelength, 1.4 cm at C band, turns an arrow by half a turn, so the arrows of a cell a few meters wide point every which way. A second pass from a nearby position changes every range by almost the same amount: the whole chain turns as one piece, and the phase of s1s2* keeps the geometry. If the objects move between the passes, the chain is rebuilt and that phase is lost. The share of the signal that survives is the coherence.

The class exercise, step by step

The Session 13 phasor card (6 October, two minutes in teams) works one resolution cell by hand. The button Load the class exercise places the card's five scatterers in the simulator at C band, with no viewpoint shift and no motion, so that each step below can be checked on screen.

  1. Add the five arrows head to tail.

    Each scatterer returns an arrow whose length is its amplitude a and whose angle is its phase φ. Arrows add by placing the tail of each on the head of the previous one, starting at the origin; the arrow from the origin to the last head is the pixel value s, and the order of the arrows does not change it. In numbers, the real parts and the imaginary parts add separately:

    The pixel has length √(0.573² + 1.000²) = 1.15 and phase atan2(1.000, 0.573) = 60°. The five arrows are 3.3 long in total, yet the pixel is only 1.15 long, because arrows that point in different directions partly cancel.

    On the pagePress Load the class exercise. With no viewpoint shift the two passes are identical: the thin arrows in the middle panel are the card's five arrows head to tail, and the orange arrow, drawn over the dashed s₁, is their sum. The readouts show |s₁| = 1.15 and arg s₁ = 60°.
  2. Move scatterer 3 away from the radar by a quarter wavelength.

    A displacement of λ/4 (1.39 cm at C band, where λ = 5.55 cm) lengthens the round trip by λ/2, so the phase of scatterer 3 changes by −4π(λ/4)/λ = −π: its arrow turns by half a turn, from 90° to 270°. Its imaginary part goes from +0.6 to −0.6, the imaginary part of the sum drops by 1.2, and the pixel becomes s = (0.573, −0.200), of length 0.61 and phase −19°. The pixel gets darker: its intensity |s|² falls from 1.33 to 0.37, a factor of 3.6, although only one object out of five moved, by 1.4 cm, in a cell 2.3 m wide.

    On the pageScatterer 3 is selected after loading (orange ring). Press Move the selected object by λ/4: its arrow flips in the middle panel and |s₁| drops to 0.61.

    The phase of a single image is set by the arrangement of the scatterers at the centimeter scale, which is never known. That is why it looks random.

  3. Observe the same cell from a slightly different position.

    Load the exercise again to undo step 2. On a second pass from a slightly farther position, every range in the cell grows by the same amount, here 0.46 cm (one sixth of λ/2), so every arrow turns by the same 60°. Turning all the arrows by one angle turns their sum by that angle, Σk ak ej(φk − 60°) = e−j60° Σk ak ejφk: the chain rotates as one piece. Its length stays 1.15, the phase of s₂ is 60° − 60° = 0°, and the phase of s₁s₂* is +60°. No redrawing is needed, and the answer is the same for any arrangement of the five scatterers.

    On the pageSet the viewpoint shift to 60°. The orange s₂ has the length of the dashed s₁ and is turned by 60°, and arg s₁s₂* reads 60°. Press New arrangement several times: |s₁| and arg s₁ change every time, arg s₁s₂* stays at 60°, and all 2,000 cells of the histogram agree.

    The phase of one image belongs to the arrangement; the phase difference of two images belongs to the geometry, provided the arrangement stays the same between the passes (Chapter 6, Section 6.1).

  4. Beyond the card: let the scatterers move.

    Press Reset to return to the default cell (a roof edge, a car, a curb, a stone, two branches and grass). With 0.5 cm of random motion at C band and only the vegetation moving, the coherence stays near 0.95, because the vegetation holds only 11% of the pixel's power. Unchecking the box lets everything move, and the coherence falls to about 0.5 (the model readout gives 0.53). At L band the same motion leaves about 0.97: the Zebker–Villasenor factor exp(−8π²σ²/λ²) depends on the motion measured in wavelengths (Chapter 6, Section 6.5).

The share of the power is ak²/Σ ak², the fraction of the pixel's mean intensity that the object contributes.

Size and color of a circle
The size is the amplitude, which is the length of the object's arrow; the color says whether the object can move between the passes (blue: rigid; green: vegetation). Amplitudes are fixed. The roof edge alone holds 43% of the power, so it dominates the pixel.
Dragging an object left or right (slant range)
This is the only change that alters an object's phase, φk = −4πRk/λ, where Rk is its distance from the radar. A move of ΔR turns its arrow by −4πΔR/λ and leaves the arrow's length unchanged: half a turn for λ/4 (1.39 cm at C band, 6.05 cm at L band) and a full turn, back to the same direction, for λ/2. A one-pixel nudge on screen moves the object by about 1 cm, which turns its arrow by about 130° at C band, 30° at L band and 230° at X band, so at C band the arrow jumps from one direction to another under the mouse. The button Move the selected object by λ/4 gives a controlled move.
Dragging an object up or down (azimuth)
Nothing changes: not the arrows, not the pixel, not the readouts. The radar measures distance along its line of sight, and in a focused image the line of sight is perpendicular to the flight direction, so sliding an object along the track leaves its distance unchanged; a 1 cm slide changes the range by less than 10−10 m. The page also gives every object the same weight wherever it sits in the cell, which is a simplification: in a real image an object near the edge of the cell contributes a little less, through the impulse response of Chapter 4.
Clicking an object
Selects it: an orange ring marks it, its arrow is drawn thicker in the middle panel, and the λ/4 button acts on it.
Move the selected object by λ/4
Moves the selected object a quarter wavelength farther in range, or nearer if it would leave the cell; either way its arrow zk turns by exactly 180°, and the pixel becomes s − 2zk. Whether it gets darker or brighter depends on how that arrow lines up with the sum.
Band
Sets the wavelength for every object: X band (TerraSAR-X, 3.1 cm), C band (Sentinel-1, 5.55 cm) or L band (NISAR, 24.2 cm). The objects stay where they are, but their phases change, because the phase counts range in units of λ/2. The same motion also costs less coherence at a longer wavelength.
Viewpoint shift (geometric phase)
How much farther the cell is from the second position than from the first, expressed as a phase. Every arrow of pass 2 turns by this same angle, so the chain rotates without changing shape: s₂ has the length of s₁, and arg s₁s₂* equals the slider value. In a real pair this is the flat-Earth and topographic phase of the pixel (Chapter 6, Sections 6.2 and 6.3). The page applies exactly the same shift to every object in the cell, so it shows no geometric decorrelation (Section 6.5).
Random motion of the moving objects, rms
Between the passes, every object allowed to move gets its own random change of range, drawn from a Gaussian with this root-mean-square value; only pass 2 is affected. Because the changes differ from object to object, the chain changes shape instead of rotating: s₂ gets a different length and phase, and in the histogram the 2,000 cells spread around the geometric phase. Each object keeps its random draw until New arrangement is pressed, so moving the slider scales one pattern of motion.

The panels and the readouts
The middle panel shows s₁, the pixel on pass 1 (dashed gray), the arrows of pass 2 head to tail (thin, colored by type) and their sum s₂ (orange). The right panel repeats the experiment on 2,000 cells that hold the same objects at random places and shows the histogram of the phase of s₁s₂*, with the geometric phase dashed. |s₁| and arg s₁ are the amplitude and the phase of the pixel on pass 1; arg s₁s₂* is the interferometric phase of the cell; geometric is the slider value. The coherence over 2,000 cells is |Σ s₁s₂*| / √(Σ|s₁|² Σ|s₂|²), the estimator of Chapter 6, Section 6.4, applied to an ensemble of cells instead of a window of pixels. The model is (1 − f) + f·exp(−8π²σ²/λ²), where f is the share of the power held by the moving objects: 11% in the default cell when only the vegetation moves, 100% when everything moves. The rigid objects keep their share of the power, and the moving ones keep theirs only in proportion to the Zebker–Villasenor factor.

The model leaves out thermal noise, the shape of the impulse response and decorrelation from the baseline itself, and it applies motion directly as a change of range; Chapter 6, Sections 6.4 and 6.5, treat each effect.

Try this

  1. With no motion, drag the geometric phase from 0° to 360°. The chain in the middle panel turns as one piece, its length does not change, and every one of the 2,000 cells agrees on the phase. Why does the phase of s₁ alone tell you nothing, while the phase of s₁s₂* tells you the geometry?
  2. Select the roof edge and move it by λ/4. Predict first: by how much does its arrow turn, and does the pixel get brighter or darker? Now do the same with a branch. Why does the brightest object decide most of the answer?
  3. Set the motion to 0.5 cm at C band with only the vegetation moving. The histogram spreads, but a peak survives at the geometric phase. What sets the height of that peak? Uncheck the box so that everything moves, and compare.
  4. Keep the motion at 1 cm and switch from C band to L band. The same leaves, moving by the same amount, barely matter to NISAR. Find the motion at which L band loses as much coherence as C band did at 0.5 cm, and check it against the ratio of the wavelengths.
  5. Press "New arrangement" several times with no motion. The pixel value jumps around (speckle), yet the phase difference stays put. Which of the two is a property of the ground and which of the arrangement?
