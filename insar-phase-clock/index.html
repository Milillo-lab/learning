Learning / Phase clock

A clock with one hand, six radars

The phase of a radar echo works like a clock with one hand. Between two passes, ground motion turns the hand, and one full turn stands for half a wavelength. The radar reads where the hand stopped but not how many times it went round. Move the ground once and watch six radars read it, each with its own clock.

The idea in one equation

If the ground moves a distance d along the line of sight between two passes, the round trip of the wave changes by 2d, and the phase turns by

Δφ = 4π d / λ,    one turn (2π) for d = λ/2,    λ = c / f

The radar measures Δφ only modulo one turn. It reads the fraction of a turn, not the number of whole turns. The length of a turn, λ/2, is set by the radar's center frequency f: 2.77 cm for Sentinel-1 at C band, 34.46 cm for BIOMASS at P band. Here the hand turns clockwise as the ground moves away from the satellite, starting at twelve o'clock on the first pass, and the colors around each dial follow the color cycle of an interferogram.

The same motion on six rulers

Unroll each clock into a tape that repeats every half wavelength. The ground's position (the black mark) falls inside one segment. The color under the mark is what the interferogram shows, and its position inside the segment is what the radar reads. Which segment it is, the radar cannot tell: from the reading alone, every segment is equally possible.

Each segment holds one full color cycle and is λ/2 long. Small ticks below the black mark: the other positions that give the same reading.

Compare the six

A radar with a short half wavelength turns its hand fast: it resolves small motions finely, but it wraps sooner, and a motion of a few centimeters between neighboring pixels is already ambiguous. A radar with a long half wavelength turns it slowly: each centimeter moves the hand less, so the same phase noise means more centimeters of error, but the count is harder to lose. Longer waves also keep their phase better through vegetation, which this page does not show.

Try this

  1. Set the motion to 2.77 cm. Which hand is back at twelve o'clock, and which hands are not? For which radar does the same motion look like no motion at all?
  2. Set 12.10 cm: the NISAR L-band hand is back at twelve o'clock. Where is the Sentinel-1 hand, and how many turns did it make? How could two radars with different wavelengths, reading the same motion, help you count turns that neither can count alone?
  3. COSMO-SkyMed and TerraSAR-X both work at X band, 9.60 and 9.65 GHz. At 1 cm their hands nearly agree. How far must the ground move before they differ by 10°? By a quarter turn? (The second answer is beyond the slider: compute it from the table.)
  4. Suppose each radar reads its hand to within 10° of phase noise. Convert that to millimeters of motion for each of the six, using the last column of the table. Which radar measures one centimeter most finely, and which is least likely to lose count?
  5. On the rulers, set 15 cm. For Sentinel-1, list three other motions that give exactly the same reading. Which of the six radars would tell them apart?

Several pages round these wavelengths differently (24 cm for NISAR L band, 9 to 10 cm for S band, 70 cm for BIOMASS); the page keeps the computed values to two decimals so that the comparisons stay exact.

Why the page asks you to guess first

Predicting before you see the answer exposes what you expected, and a wrong guess tested against a simulation is a strong prompt to revise it. Comparing several cases side by side, instead of one at a time, helps you see which feature matters (here, the half wavelength) and carry it to a new radar. Both strategies are recommended by Ambrose et al. (2023), How Learning Works, 2nd edition: predictions tested with computer simulations in Chapter 2, structured comparisons for transfer in Chapter 5.
