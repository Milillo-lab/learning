Learning / Line of sight

One number for three directions

The ground can move east, north and up at once, but an interferogram records one number per pixel: the change in distance to the satellite. Set a motion in three directions, steer the two passes that look at it, and see how much of each direction reaches the radar.

The idea in one equation

A radar measures the change in its distance to the ground, along its line of sight (LOS). A ground motion with east, north and up parts dE, dN, dU changes that distance by its projection on the unit vector that points from the ground to the satellite. For a radar that looks to the right of its flight path, with heading α (the flight direction, clockwise from north) and incidence angle θ at the ground, the change toward the satellite is

dLOS = −sin θ cos α · dE + sin θ sin α · dN + cos θ · dU

The three factors are the projection weights. Each says how many centimeters of LOS change one centimeter of motion in that direction produces. Vertical motion always counts with cos θ. Horizontal motion counts with sin θ, split between east and north by the heading. Radar satellites fly near-polar orbits, so α stays close to 0° (north) or 180° (south): at Ridgecrest both Sentinel-1 passes fly within 11° of the north-south line. There sin α is small, and so is the north weight.

Which headings hide which motion

The three weights against the heading, at the incidence angle of the pass you are editing. Dashed lines: the headings of the two passes. The north weight crosses zero at headings of 0° and 180°, where the satellite flies exactly north or south; the east weight crosses zero at 90° and 270°, headings a near-polar orbit never reaches.

The Ridgecrest numbers of Radar, Explained #2

Radar, Explained #2 says how much of 1 cm of motion the radar records at Ridgecrest, for vertical, east-west and north-south motion. Those numbers come from the descending Sentinel-1 pass of 4 and 16 July 2019 (Figure 1 of the post). The ARIA product of that pair gives, at the center of the scene, an incidence angle of 39.94° and an azimuth angle of −10.40°, the direction from the ground to the satellite measured counterclockwise from east. The heading is 180° minus that angle, 190.40°, a little west of due south. The ascending pair of 4 and 10 July 2019 gives 40.11° and −170.32°, so a heading of 350.32°.

On the pagePress Ridgecrest geometry (ARIA), then 1 cm up, 1 cm east and 1 cm north in turn: the weight table of the simulator repeats these values. The east weight changes sign between the passes, because they look from opposite sides. The north weight keeps the same sign, because both passes fly within 11° of the north-south line, one a little west of north and the other a little west of south.

Try this

  1. Set 1 cm north. Before you look at the table, predict: which pass reads more, and with which sign? Now set 5 cm north and count the fringes in each synthetic interferogram. How much north motion would one fringe of Sentinel-1 need?
  2. Set 3 cm east. The two passes now show the same pattern with opposite colors. Set 3 cm up instead: the patterns agree. How would you combine an ascending and a descending reading to separate vertical from east-west motion?
  3. Set dE = 1.2 cm and dU = 1 cm. One pass sees almost nothing. Which combination of east and up motion makes the descending pass blind, and why is there always one for each pass?
  4. Turn the heading of the descending pass from 190° to 270°, as if the satellite flew west. Which weight grows and which vanishes? What would a radar on such an orbit gain, and what would it lose?
  5. Move the incidence angle from 20° to 50°. Which weights grow, which shrink, and which stays proportional to the other?
